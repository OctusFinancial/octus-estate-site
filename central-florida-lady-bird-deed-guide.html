Educational reference only, not legal advice or a deed form. A Florida Lady Bird deed (also called an enhanced life estate deed) can be a useful way to pass a particular parcel outside probate, but it is not a substitute for individualized Florida estate-planning and title advice. Homestead, marriage, minor children, title, mortgages, Medicaid, taxes, liens, and beneficiary circumstances can all change the analysis.

How it works for a married couple

The couple signs the deed, keeps a joint enhanced life estate, and names the children as remainder beneficiaries.

First spouse dies

The life estate continues in the surviving spouse by survivorship. Nothing passes to the children yet, and there is no probate. Title is usually cleaned up by recording a certified death certificate, often with an affidavit.

Surviving spouse lives in the home

The survivor still holds the enhanced life estate and can sell, refinance, or change the remainder beneficiaries alone. The children hold only an expectancy and can't block anything.

Surviving spouse dies

The remainder vests in the children automatically, by the deed itself and outside probate. Recording the death certificate (usually with an affidavit) updates the public record so title shows the children as owners.

2The six core deed components

Florida does not publish an official statutory Lady Bird deed form or an official list of required clauses. These six components are a practical drafting-and-recording checklist; the exact language is tailored by a Florida lawyer.

Core componentWhat it establishesHow to verify it
1. Proper partiesCorrect legal identity of every current owner/grantor, their capacity and marital status, and the correct identity of every named future beneficiaryMatch every name against the existing deed and government identification; verify vesting with a title search
2. Conveyance and legal descriptionA written conveyance of the exact real property, using the complete legal description, not only the address or parcel numberCompare word for word with the vesting deed or title commitment
3. Lifetime reservationThe present owner keeps possession and use for lifeConfirm the owner remains the lifetime holder and that the transfer is timed to death, not an immediate outright transfer
4. Enhanced powersThe owner expressly keeps broad lifetime power to sell, convey, mortgage, lease, encumber, revoke, or otherwise dispose of the property without beneficiary consent, and to keep the proceedsThe attorney confirms the clause creates an enhanced life estate rather than a conventional life estate
5. Remainder and contingenciesWho receives the property at death, in what percentages, and what happens if a named beneficiary dies firstSurvivorship, descendants, substitute beneficiaries, or another contingency plan is stated clearly
6. Execution and recording informationProper signatures, two subscribing witnesses, acknowledgment/notarization, printed names and addresses, preparer information, grantee addresses, and clerk spaceUse a county recording checklist and inspect the original before submission

Statutory execution and recording baseline

Section 689.01, Florida Statutes, requires a written conveyance of a freehold interest to be signed in the presence of two subscribing witnesses.

Section 695.26, Florida Statutes, sets the information needed for recording, including printed, stamped, or typewritten names and mailing addresses for signers and witnesses, the preparer's name and address, the grantee's name and address for most deeds, and blank space reserved for the clerk at the upper right of each page.

Notarization is generally used so the instrument can be recorded and relied on in the land records. Counties generally don't correct missing information after submission, so the original is worth checking carefully first.

4County reference

Statewide note: Recording requirements under section 695.26, including printed witness names and mailing addresses, apply in every county. Deeds that don't meet them may be rejected.

5Lady Bird deed vs. revocable living trust

Both can pass Florida real estate outside probate. They differ in reach, flexibility, and ongoing management.

QuestionLady Bird deedRevocable living trust
What it coversUsually one identified parcel of real estateCan hold multiple real estate parcels and other funded assets
Control while competentOwner keeps broad control only if enhanced powers are correctly draftedGrantor typically serves as trustee and can amend or revoke while competent
Probate avoidanceFor the specifically deeded property, if the deed is valid and remains effectiveFor assets properly titled in the trust or otherwise coordinated with it
Incapacity managementLimited; it does not itself appoint a manager for the ownerSuccessor trustee can manage trust assets under stated incapacity provisions
PrivacyDeed and named beneficiaries appear in public official recordsTrust terms are generally private, though real estate deeds remain public
Multiple beneficiariesOften produces direct co-ownership at deathTrustee can sell, hold, distribute proceeds, or manage property under instructions
Beneficiary protectionLittle or none after direct distributionCan include continuing trusts and spendthrift or protective provisions
Flexibility for contingenciesPossible, but deed provisions can become complexUsually better for successor, age, special-needs, and blended-family provisions
Complexity and upfront costGenerally lower for a simple one-property planUsually higher, and requires proper funding and ongoing maintenance
Main legal riskFlorida homestead, title, or execution problems; unclear enhanced-powers language; beneficiary creditor or co-owner issuesAssets not funded into the trust; trustee selection and administration; the plan may still need a pour-over will and coordinated beneficiary designations

A Lady Bird deed may be worth discussing when

There is one uncomplicated parcel, clean title, a straightforward adult-beneficiary plan, and no need for detailed incapacity or beneficiary-protection provisions.

A revocable trust may be worth discussing when

There are multiple properties or assets, beneficiary-protection needs, blended-family concerns, minors, special-needs issues, rentals, a desire for privacy and ongoing management, or a need for a clear incapacity plan.

Legal sources to verify

  • Section 689.01, Florida Statutes: deed execution and the two-subscribing-witness requirement.
  • Section 695.26, Florida Statutes: recording requirements for instruments affecting real property, including information for signers, witnesses, the preparer, grantees, and reserved clerk space.
  • County Clerk/Comptroller Official Records resources: local recording fees, submission methods, e-recording availability, document standards, and public-records indexing.
